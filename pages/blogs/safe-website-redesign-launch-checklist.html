The Checklist for Launching a Completely Redesigned Website Safely

website redesignlaunch checklisttechnical SEOwebsite QAconversion tracking
Date: 22 Oct 26

By Webx Design Studio•0 views

A redesign should make your business feel sharper—not make customers, search engines, or your team wonder where everything went. The launch is the moment the work becomes real: URLs change, forms begin collecting leads, campaigns point to new pages, and every missed detail becomes public.

The good news is that a safe launch is not mysterious. It is a sequence of deliberate checks, with an owner, a fallback and a clear decision to proceed. Use this practical playbook to take a completely redesigned website live without losing the momentum you already earned.

What this launch checklist covers

  1. Before launch: protect what already works
  2. SEO and URL migration
  3. Experience and conversion QA
  4. The launch window
  5. The first 72 hours
  6. Who owns each check

Start with a launch plan, not a launch date

A date only tells the team when pressure will arrive. A launch plan tells everyone what “ready” means, who decides, and how to recover if something is wrong. Treat your current website as a valuable asset: it contains traffic patterns, rankings, conversion paths, customer knowledge and often years of small optimisations.

01 / PROTECT

Capture the baseline

Save a crawl, export key analytics and record the conversion events that matter before anything changes.

02 / PROVE

Test the real experience

QA the new site on actual devices, with real forms, emails, payments and edge-case content.

03 / OBSERVE

Launch with a pulse

Monitor people, search and systems after deployment—and keep rollback possible.

1. Capture what you cannot afford to lose

Before the old site is changed, create a record that lets you compare, recover and explain. Crawl the live site to produce a URL inventory, including metadata, headings, canonical tags, status codes and internal links. Export analytics for your most important pages, channels and conversion events. If a redesign changes results later, this is how you distinguish a real issue from normal variation.

Baseline checklist

  • Back up the production database, files, CMS configuration and DNS records.
  • Save a full crawl of the current site and flag high-traffic, high-value and backlink-rich URLs.
  • Export the last 3–6 months of traffic, rankings, conversions and form submissions.
  • Document current consent, cookie, CRM, email and payment integrations.
  • Agree a written rollback plan: who can trigger it, how long it takes and what it restores.
A beautiful replacement is not automatically a successful redesign. Preserve the parts of the old site that people and search engines already trust, then deliberately improve them.

2. Migrate SEO intentionally—page by page

Most redesign traffic drops are not caused by a new visual style. They happen when useful old pages vanish, redirects point to a generic homepage, metadata disappears, or internal links break. Every meaningful old URL needs a purposeful destination. A one-to-one redirect is ideal; a closely related new page is the next best option. Do not redirect unrelated pages simply to make crawl errors disappear.

SEO migration checklist

  • Map every old URL to its new equivalent and implement permanent 301 redirects before go-live.
  • Keep proven page intent, useful copy and topic relevance when consolidating pages.
  • Check titles, descriptions, headings, canonical tags, alt text, robots directives and structured data.
  • Generate a fresh XML sitemap, keep staging blocked from indexing, and confirm production is crawlable.
  • Test internal links, pagination, search, XML sitemap links and the most valuable external campaign links.

3. Test the journeys that make the website worth having

A generic “looks good” review misses the moments that create revenue and trust. Build a short test script around real visitor jobs: finding a service, understanding an offer, completing a form, booking a call, checking out, downloading a resource or getting support. Run it on the devices and browsers your audience genuinely uses.

Do this on staging with production-like settings wherever possible. Then repeat a focused version immediately after launch; integrations, cache layers, DNS and third-party tools can behave differently in the live environment.

4. Make the launch window calm and observable

Choose a window when the right people are available and the business can tolerate a small amount of focused attention. Avoid a major campaign, peak sales event or holiday period unless the launch is designed around it. Freeze non-essential changes, assign one person to coordinate decisions, and use a single shared channel for updates.

Go-live checklist

  • Confirm the production backup and rollback instructions one final time.
  • Switch DNS or deployment, then immediately validate the homepage and priority URLs on a mobile network.
  • Run a redirect sample, submit every key form and check confirmation messages.
  • Confirm SSL, canonical domain, indexability, sitemap, robots.txt and no staging links.
  • Watch error reporting, uptime, server logs and the conversion dashboard for the first hour.

5. Treat the first 72 hours as a learning window

A launch is the beginning of a release, not the end of a project. Check Search Console for indexing or coverage issues, review 404s and redirect chains, compare form volume with your baseline, and watch the highest-value landing pages. Listen to customer support and sales teams: they hear friction that dashboards cannot always describe.

  • First hour: Validate critical paths and fix clear blockers quickly.
  • First day: Review real-session errors, redirects, performance and conversion events.
  • First week: Compare traffic quality, search visibility, form completion and user feedback with the saved baseline.
  • First month: Turn launch observations into a prioritised optimisation backlog instead of declaring the redesign “finished.”

6. Give every checkbox an owner

Checklists work only when someone is accountable for each line. Your designer can own experience QA, a developer can own deployment and performance, a marketer can own redirects and measurement, and a stakeholder can make the final business call. For a smaller team, one person may wear several hats—but the ownership should still be explicit.

The safest launch question is not “Are we ready?” It is: “Have we checked the things that would hurt our customers or business if they failed, and do we know what to do next?” If the answer is yes, you have earned a confident go-live.
